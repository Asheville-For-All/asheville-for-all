Letter to Asheville Planning and Zoning Commission

by Asheville For All
November 4, 2026 Updated: November 6, 2026

UPDATE: As predicted, the topic of a moratorium on land use code change did come up at the Nov. 4 Asheville Planning and Zoning Commission meeting.

You can read all about the discussion in this article from 828 News Now.

Asheville For All sent the following letter to Asheville’s Planning and Zoning Commission ahead of their November 4th meeting.

We had received word that the commission may be discussing the possibility of a city-imposed moratorium on interim changes to Asheville’s land use code while that code is wholly rewritten—a process expected to take several years.

There will not be any votes or decisions made at this meeting. Nevertheless, we joined others in taking the opportunity to encourage the Planning and Zoning Commission to push back against the idea of a moratorium.

* * *

Dear Asheville Planning and Zoning Commissioners,

We write to express concern about the idea that further “interim” land use reforms will be off the table in Asheville while the city conducts its planned UDO rewrite.

It’s our understanding that this may come under discussion this Wednesday at your November meeting. We hope that you will take the opportunity to express support for further interim changes akin to those that the city passed this summer.

In this concern, we join several Asheville City Council members who expressed the desire to maintain the possibility for more interim updates at their September 3rd agenda briefing.

We are especially concerned about land use as it pertains to the city’s housing shortage.

Despite recent editorializing in the media, Asheville has not “overbuilt” housing. HUD reports that rents are expected to rise in the area, and Harvard reports that Asheville faces near record high price-to-income ratios for housing.

If history is any indication, we expect that the timeline for completion of the new UDO will stretch far beyond what is projected. And even when land use reforms are adopted, changes in policy do not manifest in changes to the built environment overnight. We would prefer to see change come sooner rather than later.

Furthermore, in cities across the country, including nearby Durham and Atlanta, we’ve seen comprehensive zoning reforms get bogged down in legal challenges and political backlashes. The case of Austin, TX is instructive. After its ambitious “Code Next” project got stuck in the courts, the city declared it DOA. Then the city government simply passed smaller, piecemeal reforms in subsequent years. It proved to be a less unwieldy and more agile strategy for the city.

We are not arguing against the UDO overhaul, and we are excited to see what it brings. But we think that a fail-safe approach should include smaller, incremental changes alongside it.

And as a matter of fact, this is the exact approach that the city’s 2024 Affordable Housing Plan calls for. On page 68 of the document, it suggests that “missing middle” related reforms might be given “top priority” at the same time that a complete UDO rewrite is undertaken on a different timeline.

We understand the desire not to complicate matters during the UDO rewrite. We strongly encourage the city, the Planning and Zoning Commission, and the Asheville City Council to entertain reforms that can be implemented broadly and simply, for example, diminishing or eliminating lot size minimums.

Thanks as always for all that you do,
Asheville For All Lead Organizers
